# Article 2 — The Champion Problem
## "Your Biggest Threat in an Enterprise AI Deal Is Not the Competition"

**Publish:** Week 3  
**Length:** ~1,900 words  
**Why second:** Founders and anyone who has done enterprise sales will immediately recognize this. It's specific, uncomfortable, and actionable. High share potential.

---

## FULL ARTICLE TEXT

---

I have lost deals to internal politics more often than I have lost them to competing products.

Not because our product was worse. Not because we were priced wrong. Because the person inside the organization who believed in what we were building — who had fought for the budget, run the evaluation, told their leadership it was worth doing — quietly lost the internal credibility to carry it across the line.

This is the thing nobody in enterprise AI sales talks about honestly, because it's uncomfortable to acknowledge. Your biggest risk after a successful proof of concept is not a competitor with better features. It's the fragility of a single human relationship inside an organization you don't control.

---

### How Champion Fragility Actually Works

The standard enterprise sales model assumes a champion is stable. You identify the internal advocate, you enable them with talking points and ROI data and executive briefings, you trust them to move the deal through the organization. This works until it doesn't.

What makes a champion fragile is not their commitment to your product. It's their standing inside their own organization. And that standing is always in flux — affected by things that have nothing to do with you.

A champion can lose internal credibility in a lot of ways. Their budget gets cut in a planning cycle, and suddenly they don't have the authority they did when they started championing the initiative. A reorg happens and they now report to someone who has different priorities. They field a question from a risk or compliance stakeholder and don't handle it well, and now they're seen as someone who didn't do their homework. A competing internal initiative gets resourced and theirs loses momentum. They get pulled into a higher-priority project and the AI initiative becomes something they're maintaining rather than driving.

None of these developments are visible to you until the deal has already slowed down. By the time you notice that responses are taking longer, meetings are harder to schedule, and next steps are vague — the problem has usually been developing for weeks.

---

### The Warning Signs (That Arrive Too Late If You're Not Watching)

There are patterns that signal champion fragility before the deal visibly stalls. I've learned to watch for these.

**Their update cadence changes.** Early in a deal, a strong champion is proactive — they reach out with news, questions, context. When the cadence shifts from proactive to reactive, and then from reactive to slow, something has changed internally. It's rarely the product.

**They stop sharing internal context.** A confident champion tells you what's happening in the organization — who's asking questions, what the risk team is concerned about, where the budget conversation landed. When that information stops flowing, it often means they're no longer in the rooms where those conversations are happening.

**The deal scope gets narrowed.** A full deployment becomes a pilot. A pilot becomes a "limited evaluation." Each narrowing is often a champion trying to get something approved that their depleted internal capital can actually carry — rather than admitting the original scope isn't movable right now.

**They start asking you for things they should already have.** ROI calculations you provided months ago. Documentation they've already seen. References they could have requested in week two. This is often someone rebuilding a case they've already made, for a new audience they've had to start over with.

---

### What You Can Actually Do

The tactical answer is multi-threading — identifying and developing multiple relationships inside the account so that no single person is the single point of failure. This is correct and most people already know it. The harder question is how to do this without undermining the champion who got you in the door.

The honest answer: you can't completely thread this needle. Champions are territorial by necessity. They got you access, they're running political risk on your behalf, and they're sensitive to anything that looks like you going around them. If you're too aggressive about building direct relationships with their peers and supervisors, you can damage the relationship that's keeping you alive.

What has worked for me is being explicit with the champion about why multi-threading helps them, not just you. The framing: "The more people I can directly answer questions for, the less you have to spend your time fielding questions internally. Let me help you by having direct conversations with the risk team / compliance / your IT security counterpart, so that you're not the conduit for every conversation."

Most champions, once they understand this is about reducing their load rather than replacing their role, are receptive. And practically, it means you get direct access to the stakeholders whose specific objections are going to determine whether this moves.

In parallel: build the paper trail that doesn't require the champion to be present. Thorough documentation, clear model validation materials, regulatory position papers that a risk officer can read independently. The champion can distribute these without being in every conversation. And if the champion is suddenly less available, there's a body of work that continues to do the selling even when the relationship is under pressure.

### The Situation I Didn't See Coming

We were well into a deal — past the POC, into commercial discussions — when the champion took on a new role. Not a bad role, a lateral move that was actually a promotion in some ways. But the new role had different scope, and the AI initiative was in his old scope.

He didn't disappear. He still showed up on calls. He still said the right things. But he was no longer the person whose annual review depended on this succeeding. He had become, without announcing it, a sponsor — someone who supports the thing in principle, but whose calendar and political capital are committed elsewhere.

The deal didn't die immediately. It drifted. Slowly, then all at once.

By the time we understood what had happened, the internal momentum we'd built over eight months had dissipated. There was no single person who felt the urgency anymore. We were back to effectively restarting the internal case, with a new champion who hadn't been through the process and needed to be rebuilt from scratch.

We eventually closed it, but twelve months later than projected and at a smaller initial scope. The cost wasn't just the time — it was the opportunity cost of the team hours spent re-building a case that had already been built once.

---

### The Structural Change That Matters

The most important thing I've changed about how I approach enterprise deals is this: I now try to create a situation where the business case lives independently of any single champion.

This means doing more upfront work to get formal alignment — not informal enthusiasm, but formal sign-off from multiple stakeholders — before the deal progresses past the evaluation stage. It means insisting on exec-level involvement earlier, even when the champion says it's not necessary yet. It means creating documentation that any new stakeholder could read cold and understand the rationale for moving forward.

It also means having an explicit conversation with the champion early about succession. Not "what happens if you leave" — that's too pointed. More like: "As this moves forward, who else should we make sure is closely engaged so that organizational changes don't create delays?" Most people will tell you exactly who the other decision-makers are. And that gives you the map you need to build relationships that outlast any single person.

---

There is a version of enterprise sales that treats the champion as the solution. You find them, you enable them, you trust them to deliver. This works in short sales cycles where the champion's role doesn't change before the deal closes.

In long enterprise sales cycles — the kind that are normal in regulated financial services — the champion is not a solution. They are a starting point. The actual work is building a web of relationships, documentation, and formal alignment that can survive the inevitable organizational changes that will happen over a twelve-to-eighteen month sales cycle.

The deals that close in this environment are the ones that were never really dependent on a single person to begin with.
